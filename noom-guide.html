The Complete Noom Guide

The full guide. Prices and trial terms checked 20 November 2026 against the official pages listed in Sources at the end of this article, except the MyFitnessPal price, checked live 22 November 2026. Anything we could not verify on an official page is marked “To be verified”.

The short answer: Noom is a behaviour-change weight-loss app built around daily psychology lessons, a colour-coded food system (green, yellow, orange), and a goal coach. It costs $70 a month on the shortest plan and $209 for 12 months paid up front (checked 20 November 2026), and every plan starts with a 7-day initial trial. It fits people who want structure and daily lessons without strict meal plans or group meetings. It is more than a calorie-counting app, adding daily lessons and a coach to the food log; it is not a meal-delivery service or a medication program — and this guide covers Noom Weight only, not the separate clinical service Noom Med. We promise no results and no timelines: this guide describes what the program asks you to do, what it costs, and what happens when you cancel.

It is not a diet in the usual sense: it does not hand you a meal plan, ban food groups, or deliver food to your door. It is a course of short daily lessons about why you eat the way you do, plus a food log, a daily calorie range, and a coach you can message. Whether that is worth $70 a month — or $209 for a year — depends on whether you will actually do the lessons and the logging. This guide works through the program the way we work through every program: price and trial terms, what you actually do each day, coaching, food tracking, and cancelling, with the weaknesses named alongside the strengths.

This guide covers the standard, non-clinical plan, Noom Weight. It is not medical advice. If you have a medical condition, talk to a clinician before starting a weight-loss program. For adults 18 and over only.

What is Noom?

Noom Weight is a weight-loss program that helps you change your habits and mindset around food. That sentence is Noom’s own description, and it matters, because it tells you what the program is not: it is not a meal plan, not a set of pre-packaged foods, and not a fad diet or an elimination diet. Noom’s point is that weight loss is not just about what you eat, but why you eat — and that most diets fail because they only tell you what to eat, not how to change the habits behind your choices.

The program is built on psychology. Its lessons are based on cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), which Noom describes as a type of therapy proven to help people change habits and behaviour. The lessons cover what triggers you to eat when you are not hungry, how to overcome emotional and stress eating, what to do instead of reaching for a snack when you do not feel good, how to incorporate mindfulness while eating, how to choose foods that keep you full, and how to tell genuine hunger apart from “head hunger” (checked 20 November 2026).

  • You start by answering questions on Noom’s website about your current weight, your goal weight, and your experience with dieting and weight loss. It takes about 10 minutes (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You then receive a plan — usually for around 16 weeks, longer or shorter depending on your goal — with a suggested daily calorie range (checked 20 November 2026).
  • From day one, daily lessons of around 5–15 minutes, with exercises, quizzes, or tasks (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You log everything you eat in the app, after each meal or at the end of the day — your choice — and you can weigh in daily if you want to (checked 20 November 2026).
  • A coach and guides are included, and there is a community of other members available around the clock (checked 20 November 2026).
  • A database of thousands of recipes is included — a recipe library, not a set meal plan (checked 20 November 2026).
  • No food is off limits. You can eat cake, chips, or curry — you log them and aim to stay within your calorie range (checked 20 November 2026).

Noom also states plainly what it is not: it is not pre-packaged food you depend on for the rest of your life, and it is not a program that cuts out whole food groups. The Mediterranean diet is Noom’s own example of a healthy, science-backed way of eating that still often fails — because it only says “eat this, not that”, which is hard to keep up. Noom’s bet is that step-by-step changes to your eating habits and lifestyle add up to a bigger difference than a food list does (checked 20 November 2026).

How Noom works, day by day

Daily lessons

From day one, you get a daily lesson of around 5–15 minutes. Each lesson comes with exercises, quizzes, or tasks, and the content is built on behavioural science — cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) above all. The lessons are the part of Noom that a tracker cannot replace: they are meant to give you “lightbulb moments” about your own eating — why you reach for food when you are stressed, how to tell real hunger from head hunger, what to do when your weight stalls — that you internalise and keep after the program ends (checked 20 November 2026).

The time commitment is small on paper — less than 15 minutes a day, by Noom’s own description — but it is daily, and it is the part most likely to be skipped when life gets busy. If you will not read a lesson a day, you are paying for the part of Noom you will not use.

Coaching and support

A coach and guides are included in the program. Noom says its coaches are trained in the science behind behaviour change, adapt to how often you want to check in — once every couple of weeks is fine, or more often if you are struggling — can look at your food log and suggest different foods, and can give more intensive help when you hit a plateau (checked 20 November 2026). There is also a community of other members, available around the clock, and Noom’s own example of how it helps is asking other members what works when you want Chinese food and are not sure what to choose.

Two things about coaching we could not verify, and so we do not state them as facts: how quickly a coach replies, and whether coaching is one-to-one messaging or group-based. Both are to be verified. If the speed and format of coaching matter to your decision, check them on Noom’s official help pages or by asking during the trial — do not assume a coach is on call.

Food logging and your calorie budget

You log everything you eat in the app, after every meal or snack, or once at the end of the day — your choice. Noom gives you a suggested daily calorie range when your plan is set up, based on your answers, and a suggested daily allotment for each food colour. The more you track, the likelier you are to reach your goal weight, according to research Noom cites — but the logging itself is work, every day, and the program is only as good as the logging you actually do (checked 20 November 2026).

You also get the option to weigh in every day. Noom says daily weigh-ins are optional, and that small day-to-day fluctuations are normal and part of almost every weight-loss story. The point of weighing daily, in Noom’s account, is to make the fluctuations boring rather than alarming — a weekly weigh-in can turn one bad number into a big deal (checked 20 November 2026).

Exercise

Exercise is not required to start. Noom says it starts with food when it comes to weight loss, and suggests starting with 200 steps a day when you are ready, building up as your confidence builds. The app includes a pedometer and exercise tracking, and it syncs with Apple Watch, Fitbit, and Garmin, plus iHealth, Misfit, Omron, Polar, Qardio, Runkeeper, Withings, and Yoo (checked 20 November 2026). The order matters: Noom’s position is that changing what you eat comes first, because that is what gives you the results that motivate you to build exercise in afterwards.

What a typical day looks like

Read a lesson — around 5–15 minutes — at whatever time suits you. Log what you eat, either after each meal or once at the end of the day; Noom leaves the timing to you. Step on the scale if you want to — daily weigh-ins are optional, and Noom says the small day-to-day fluctuations are normal. Check in with your coach as often or as rarely as you like: once every couple of weeks is fine, or more often when you are struggling or have hit a plateau. If you are ready for exercise, start with 200 steps a day and build up; if you are not ready, the program starts with food and waits (checked 20 November 2026).

That is the whole daily ask: a short lesson, logging, and an optional weigh-in. There is no meal to cook from a plan, no meeting to attend, and no food group to avoid. The ask is small, but it is daily — and the program only works if the small ask actually happens.

The food colour system: green, yellow, orange

Every food you log is categorised green, yellow, or orange by calorie density — how much of it you would need to eat to feel satisfied. Green foods pack the fewest calories for their bulk: most vegetables, some fruits, and whole grains sit here. Yellow covers the middle ground: lean meats, starches, and healthy fats such as avocados and legumes. Orange holds the foods with the most calories per bite, or the fewest nutrients — cheeseburgers and desserts are Noom’s examples (checked 20 November 2026).

Noom suggests roughly 30% of daily intake from green foods, 45% from yellow, and 25% from orange (checked 20 November 2026). Orange does not mean “bad”, and no food is off limits — the colours are a guide to portions, not a ban list. The point of the system is calorie density: half a pound of peanut butter contains 1,334 calories, while half a pound of spinach contains 52, and half a pound of oatmeal has 154 calories but still sits on the green list because it is packed with belly-filling fibre (checked 20 November 2026).

One note on wording: some older articles call the third colour red. Noom’s current official pages call it orange, and this guide follows the current official wording.

Who Noom may suit — and who it may not

Noom’s own page lists who the program is for, and the list is a useful starting point because it is written for a specific reader rather than everyone (checked 20 November 2026). This section follows that list and adds the practical reading from the rest of this guide.

Noom may suit you if…

  • You have tried weight-loss programs before and could not keep the weight off, and you want a different starting point — Noom’s own list starts with exactly that reader (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You want to understand why you eat — triggers, stress, boredom — rather than just count what you eat.
  • You would rather not meet with a group every week (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You want a more structured program, with daily tasks, challenges, and lessons to keep you on track, and clear steps to follow every day rather than being left on your own (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You are busy and have 5–15 minutes a day for a lesson, but not time for complicated meal prep or meetings.
  • You eat out often: you can eat your favourite foods, including restaurant meals — you log them and stay within your calorie range, and the community can help with specific choices (checked 20 November 2026).
  • You are vegetarian or follow another way of eating: no food is off limits and Noom says the program fits every lifestyle, with your coach helping you fit it to how you eat (checked 20 November 2026). The colour system works by calorie density, not by food group, so it does not depend on eating meat. Note: the official pages do not name vegetarian plans specifically; this is our reading of their general statement.
  • You eat for emotional reasons — stress, anxiety, boredom — and want lessons about that rather than another calorie counter. The lessons cover what triggers you to eat when you are not hungry and what to do instead of reaching for a snack when you do not feel good (checked 20 November 2026).
  • Your schedule is irregular: the program is app-based, the lessons take 5–15 minutes, and you log when it suits you — after each meal or once at the end of the day. Noom does not publish a schedule built for shift work specifically, so this is our reading of how the format works, not a claim from an official page.

Noom may not suit you if: you dislike logging everything you eat, every day — logging is a core part of the program, not optional. Or if the up-front payment puts you off: $169 for four months or $209 for a year is charged in one go. Or if you want face-to-face group support, which Noom does not offer. Or if you want a meal plan handed to you — Noom gives you a calorie range and colour guidance, not a menu.

Noom vs WeightWatchers and MyFitnessPal

This guide is about Noom, so the comparison here is short. The full side-by-side — same five points for each program, with the weaknesses of the recommended program named — is WeightWatchers vs Noom: an honest comparison. In one paragraph: WeightWatchers is built around its Points system and workshops, and is the cheapest entry point of the paid programs; MyFitnessPal is a tracker, not a program, and is the cheapest paid option overall. Noom is the only one of the three built around changing how you think about food, with daily lessons and an included coach — and it carries the highest headline price and the longest lock-in through lump-sum payments.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths: the only program of the three built around changing how you think about food, with daily CBT-based lessons; a coach is included rather than sold as an upgrade; no food is banned; lessons take only 5–15 minutes a day; you can eat out and follow any way of eating; the community is available around the clock.

Weaknesses: the most expensive headline price — $70 a month if you pay month to month — and multi-month plans are charged in full up front, which is a real commitment before you know whether you will stick with it. Daily logging of everything you eat is a chore, and the program is only as good as the logging you actually do. The 7-day trial is short: a week is enough to try the lessons, not to judge a habit. Coaching reply times and whether the coach is one-to-one are still to be verified. And the trial fee itself is still to be verified — the official page we read does not state one.

Common objections, answered

“I don’t like logging food.” Then be honest with yourself: Noom asks you to log everything you eat, every day. The colour system makes the logging more informative than a bare calorie count, but it does not make it shorter. If logging itself is the problem, no version of Noom will fix it — the lightest version of any program is a tracker with no lessons, and even that asks you to log.

“I don’t want group meetings.” Noom is app-only, and Noom explicitly lists “you’d rather not have to meet with a group every week” as a reason it may fit you (checked 20 November 2026). There is a community you can read and post in, but no meeting to attend.

“I’ve tried apps before and quit.” That is the reader Noom is written for — its own description starts with people who have tried weight-loss programs and could not keep the weight off (checked 20 November 2026). What is different is the structure: daily lessons about triggers and stress eating, plus a coach, rather than a bare calorie counter. What is not different is the daily work: you still log everything, and a week’s trial will not tell you whether you will still be logging in month three.

“Is the free trial really free?” Noom’s official page describes a 7-day initial trial and does not state a trial fee — whether a fee applies is to be verified. In every case, check the trial end date on the day you sign up: Noom’s plans auto-renew.

“What does it really cost, and what am I locked into?” Noom: $70 a month, or $169 for four months / $209 for twelve months paid up front, auto-renewing. All checked 20 November 2026. The lock-in is the lump-sum payment: you pay the whole term before you know whether you will stick with it, and the plan renews for another term unless you cancel.

“Is this a real programme or a fad?” Noom is a behaviour-change program built on CBT-based lessons, not a diet that cuts out food groups. That is a description of method, not a promise about your outcome — treat the method as the thing to judge, and judge it on whether you will do the daily lessons and logging.

Our verdict

Our verdict: Noom is worth trying for the reader it is written for — someone who has tried diets before, wants to change how they think about food, and does not want group meetings. The daily lessons and included coach match that reader’s problem more directly than a Points budget or a bare tracker, and the 5–15 minute daily commitment fits a busy schedule. Go in with your eyes open about its weaknesses: $70 a month is a high headline price, the cheaper rates mean paying $169 or $209 in one go, the 7-day trial is short, the trial fee is still to be verified, and the whole program depends on you logging everything, every day.

Do not choose Noom if you only want tracking — a tracker costs less and asks less of you — or if you want face-to-face group support, a meal plan handed to you, or a program that involves a clinician.
